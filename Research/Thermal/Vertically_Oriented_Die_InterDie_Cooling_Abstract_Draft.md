# Comparative Thermal Analysis of Inter-Die Heat-Path Cooling and Direct-to-Silicon Liquid Cooling in Vertical-Die (V-die) DRAM

**Status:** Working draft updated after clarification of the core comparison (2026-10-12). The draft addresses two V-die cooling architectures: inter-die heat-path cooling with Cu heat-path layers and a top-mounted cold plate, and direct-to-silicon liquid cooling through inter-die gaps containing Cu pillars. HBM provides background motivation, not the direct cooling benchmark. The main objective is to identify the die-count and per-die heat-load limits of both architectures under a defined DRAM temperature ceiling. Detailed comparison conditions and study ranges remain to be confirmed.

## Abstract

The growing demand for data processing requires higher memory capacity and bandwidth from artificial intelligence (AI) accelerators. High-bandwidth memory (HBM) increases DRAM capacity through die stacking, whereas the routing area available for TSV-based I/O constrains further bandwidth scaling. As additional dies and bonding layers are stacked, heat must conduct through the stack, increasing thermal resistance and limiting further scaling [1], [2]. To address these coupled scaling constraints, vertically oriented die (V-die) architectures have been proposed [3], [4]. Upright DRAM dies use side-edge pads for I/O; adding dies can therefore increase memory capacity and the number of I/O connections, supporting higher bandwidth. Heat generated in each upright DRAM die is conducted in-plane through silicon toward a top-mounted cold plate. In the inter-die heat-path cooling configuration, Cu heat-path layers fill the gaps between adjacent dies and conduct heat along the die height toward the cold plate, with no coolant flowing through the gaps. The Cu layers also thermally couple neighboring dies, and the thermal limit of this solid heat path as die count and per-die power increase remains to be quantified. Comparative simulations quantify the thermal limits of inter-die heat-path cooling and direct-to-silicon liquid cooling, which circulates coolant through the inter-die gaps to remove heat from the die surfaces.

The direct-to-silicon liquid-cooling architecture circulates coolant through the gaps between adjacent upright DRAM dies and removes heat directly from their passivated surfaces (**Figure 1(b)**). Cu pillars span the gaps, mechanically supporting neighboring dies and maintaining coolant passages. The pillars also conduct heat between adjacent dies, forming solid thermal bridges, while their coolant-exposed surfaces add area for heat exchange. During wafer-level stacking, Cu pillars are thermocompression-bonded to Cu landing pads on the backside of the overlying DRAM wafer. After dicing and vertical mounting on an interposer, the inter-wafer gaps become inter-die coolant passages. The coolant-facing DRAM surfaces and exposed Cu-pillar sidewalls are conformally passivated, while the Cu-Cu bond interfaces remain uncoated. This bonding and selective-passivation sequence is a proposed process route and remains to be validated for the target geometry.

Comparative thermal simulations evaluate the inter-die heat-path and direct-to-silicon liquid-cooling architectures under matched [die count], [per-die heat load], and [boundary conditions]. Maximum die temperature (*T*max) and die-temperature distribution quantify the thermal response of each architecture. The die count and per-die heat load are then varied to determine the maximum stack size and thermal load each cooling architecture can support below a specified DRAM temperature limit ([T_lim]). For the liquid-cooling cases, coolant pressure drop (Δ*p*) and pumping power quantify the hydraulic cost. A thermal test vehicle (TTV) with passivated silicon dies and bonded Cu-pillar spacers is used to validate the local direct-cooling structure and model; infrared thermography measures accessible die-surface temperatures, and inlet/outlet pressure measurements quantify pressure drop under coolant flow.

The comparative simulations quantify the difference in *T*max and die-temperature distribution between the two V-die cooling architectures.
